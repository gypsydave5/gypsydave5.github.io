# The Incremental and the Iterative

Alistair Cockburn has a mug that says:

> Incremental (development) supports the process  
> Iterative (development) supports the product

I'm not paying to find out what that means. But let's work it out ourselves.

## What's the Difference?

**Iterative development**: Each time you deliver a *working version* of the Mona Lisa
- First iteration: a rough sketch
- Second iteration: better proportions, some shading
- Third iteration: refined details, better colors
- Each version is a *complete* (if rough) Mona Lisa

**Incremental development**: Each time you deliver a *piece* of the Mona Lisa
- First increment: just the smile
- Second increment: add the eyes
- Third increment: add the forehead
- You only get a complete Mona Lisa at the end

## Why Does This Matter?

### Iterative Supports the Product

With iterative development, you're getting feedback on the *whole thing* at each stage. You can:
- Test your assumptions with real users
- Discover you've already "won" (it's good enough)
- Change direction based on what you learn
- Validate that you're solving the right problem

Each iteration is usable, even if it's not perfect. You ship something that works, learn from it, and improve.

### Incremental Supports the Process

Incremental development helps you manage the *work*:
- Breaks down a large problem into manageable chunks ("knife and forks" it)
- Makes planning easier (you know what pieces you need)
- Lets you parallelize work across a team
- Gives you clear progress markers

But here's the catch: increments aren't necessarily usable until they're assembled. The smile alone doesn't help anyone.

## The Tension

Most teams need both:
- **Incremental** to manage complexity and coordinate work
- **Iterative** to learn and adapt to user needs

The danger is being purely incremental—building pieces without ever stopping to ask "does this *whole thing* actually work for users?" You end up with a perfectly executed plan for the wrong product.

The best approach? Incremental *within* iterations. Break the work into pieces, but make sure each iteration delivers something complete enough to learn from.
