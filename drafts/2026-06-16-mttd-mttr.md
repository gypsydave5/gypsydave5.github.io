I now "manage" a team at work. This involves a lot of exciting new changes in my life, mostly involving who now takes me more seriously, and who shouts at me when things go wrong.

Not that the shouting ever really happens — we're all very friendly folk at PLACE-OF-WORK. But sometimes I'm caught by some humbling and humiliating noise.

When, for instance, Tim turns to me in his chair and says "did you know that the submissions app is down?", reader, I did _not_ know the submissions app was down. I was doing something else. And when a colleague from another team says "did you know that you haven't been sending us drafts since 4 o'clock yesterday morning?", _mea maxima culpa_, I was equally ignorant.

And humiliated. It's _my_ system — I am, sadly, responsible for what it does. So if my chief way of knowing that something is wrong with it is someone outside the team complaining (engineers, product owners, or — worst of all — actual users) then I feel pretty stupid.

When we find out something is broken, we can actually fix it pretty quickly. Our team practises continuous delivery, we have excellent logs and metrics, the codebase is clean and easy to work on. We can fix stuff quickly.

But it's still embarrassing to have customer support call you up to say that your system is broken. No, I should be calling _them_ to tell them it's broken.

## Running With Scissors

Here's the tempting response to all this: _never, ever do anything wrong again_. Wouldn't that be better?

No. It would not be better. Software development is like running with scissors. It is inherently sharp and dangerous because of the key tenet of agile software development:

> nobody knows what they're doing

Product managers don't know what we need to make, UX don't know how our users work, engineers don't know the right way to build it. In our complex environments, we never know the total effect of our code changes. There will always be errors. There will always be changes that break the system.

## Two Strategies

There _is_ a school of thought that says: slow down, make errors less likely, and you'll have fewer problems. This is the waterfall instinct. Heavy process, extensive reviews, long testing phases — all aimed at preventing failures from reaching production. In reliability engineering terms, this is optimising for **Mean Time To Failure** (MTTF): make failures rarer by making the time between them longer.

The problem is that this doesn't actually eliminate errors. What it usually does is pile them all up into one big batch of problems that get released all at once. Discontinuous deployment, discontinuous pain. And then when something _does_ go wrong — and it will — you've optimised your development process for slowness. You can't fix quickly what you've built to deploy slowly.

Agile and XP take a different position. They say: fine, errors are a constant. Accept it. If you cannot stop failures, then what you want is to recover from them _fast_. Don't optimise for MTTF. Optimise for **Mean Time To Recovery** (MTTR).

And crucially, the practices that help you recover fast — TDD, continuous delivery, clean code — are the same practices that also make failures less likely in the first place. You get both. Which is nice.

So: great, we're doing agile, we practice continuous delivery, we write tests. Why is Tim telling me that no submissions have been added since 4 o'clock yesterday?

## MTTR Is Two Things

Recovery isn't one step, it's two:

1. You have to _detect_ that something is wrong
2. You have to _fix_ it

The time from failure to detection is **Mean Time To Detection** (MTTD). The time from detection to fix is the repair time. MTTR is the sum of both. And the distinction matters, because they require completely different tools and habits.

When Tim tells me my app is broken, my detection time is "since Tim noticed". That is terrible detection. What I want is to know about failures faster than Tim — faster than anyone else in the world, ideally. That's a detection problem.

Once I know, though? We're fine. Good logs, clean codebase, fast pipeline. We can fix things. That's repair, and our team is reasonably good at it.

## Detection: Alerts and Monitoring

If you have metrics but no alerts, you have a dashboard. Dashboards are great. Dashboards require someone to stare at them. Nobody stares at dashboards all day.

What you need is an alert that fires when no submissions have been received in the last five minutes. This is easy to configure in Grafana or whatever you're using. The hard bit isn't the technology. The hard bit is knowing _what to alert on_.

The guiding principle: alert on symptoms, not causes. "No submissions in five minutes" is a symptom. "Database CPU at 80%" is a cause. The symptom is what matters to users; the cause is what you investigate _after_ you know there's a problem.

There is also a discipline problem. I've had the alerts. I've ignored the alerts. Why? Because the alerts channel was a firehose. Every noisy, badly-tuned rule pumping constant alerts into Teams meant that when a real one arrived, it looked the same as everything else.

The fire alarm that cries wolf gets switched off.

So: clean up your alert channel. Ruthlessly. An alert should be an exceptional, unambiguous, this-needs-action-right-now event. If it isn't that, it shouldn't be an alert.

## Repair: Logs, Tests, and Confidence

Once you know something is broken, you need to do two things: find it, and fix it.

Finding it is what good logs and metrics are for. Not just "something went wrong" but "this request failed at this point, with this error, from this user, at this time." Structured logs, correlation IDs, sensible log levels. When the alert fires and you run to the dashboard, you should be able to get to the root cause in minutes, not hours.

Fixing it requires something that often gets undervalued: _confidence_. You can find the bug in ten minutes and then spend two hours nervously checking whether your fix is safe to deploy. That's waste. That's MTTR ballooning not because the problem was hard but because your pipeline doesn't give you enough certainty.

This is where tests come back in. Not just as a way to catch bugs before production — though they do that too — but as a confidence machine. A green test suite on a clean CI build means you can deploy your fix and mean it. You're not guessing. Tests are what let you go from "I think this fixes it" to "I know this fixes it" in under ten minutes, then push.

Fast, confident pipelines aren't a luxury. They're load-bearing infrastructure for your MTTR.

## Capture the Numbers

So how do you know if this is getting better?

Vibes don't scale. Counting how many times Tim has shamed you this month is a start, but it's not a metric.

What you need is to record, for each incident:

1. When did it go wrong
2. When did you detect it
3. When did you fix it

Stuff that in your incident reports — YAML front matter in markdown, if that's how you roll — and you can start to aggregate. MTTD and MTTR become numbers you can track, compare, and improve.

Break it down by severity too. Under the P1–P4 model[^priority], a P1 affecting every user should have a detection time measured in minutes. A P4 affecting Gary in Indiana and his dark mode preferences can wait. Different targets, different alerting strategies, different acceptable MTTR.

The moment you have these numbers, you stop arguing about vibes. You have data. And if the data shows your MTTD is three hours for P1s, you know exactly what to work on.

## One More Thing

On the subject of AI assistants making us "faster" — and I think we can translate "faster" as "we will introduce more changes into production" — there is a corollary. Given our already-professed ignorance, we will also introduce errors faster. More velocity, more entropy.

If you're deploying more often, MTTF will fall. That's fine, if MTTR falls with it. But if you've added AI-assisted velocity without also tightening detection and repair, then you've just given yourself a higher error rate and the same slow recovery. That's worse.

Optimise for MTTR. It's the right target regardless of how fast you're moving. It gets more important the faster you move.

[^priority]: P1: everyone affected, everything broken. P2: many users, critical path impaired. P3: some users, workaround exists. P4: edge case, negligible impact. The tricky bit is the middle — when in doubt, escalate.
