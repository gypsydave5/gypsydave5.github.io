I now "manage" a team at work. This involves a lot of exciting new changes in my life, mostly involing who now takes me more seriously, and who shouts at me when things go wrong.

Not that the shouting ever really happens - we're all very friendly folk at PLACE-OF-WORK. But sometimes I'm caught by some humbling and humiliating noise.

When, for instance, Tim turns to me in his chair and says "did you know that the submissions app is down?", reader, I did _not_ know the submissions app was down. I was doing something else. And when a colleague from another team and says "did you know that you haven't been sending us drafts since 4 o'clock yesterday morning?", _mea maxima culpa_, I was equally ignorant.

And humiliated. It's _my_ system - I am, sadly, responsible for what it does. So if my chief way of knowing that something is wrong with it is someone outside the team complaining (engineers, product owners, or - worst of all - actual users) then I feel pretty stupid.

When we find out something is broken, we can actually fix it pretty quickly. Our team practices continuous delivery, we have excellent logs and metrics, the codebase is clean and easy to work on. We can fix stuff quickly.

But it's still embarassing to have customer support call you up to say that your system is broken. No, I should be calling _them_ to tell them its broken.

So I, as an engineering manager, wanted to do something about this. Look, I have superpowers now, people have to listen to me. What I wanted to know is how long it takes for me to find out that everything is totally broken. And ideally I'd like that time to be shorter than the time it takes for _anyone else in the world_ to notice.

But wait there - this is silly - what I really want to do is _never, ever do anything wrong again_. Wouldn't that be better?

## Running With Scissors

No that would not be better. Software development is like running with scissors. It is inherently sharp and dangerous because of the key tenet of agile software development:

> nobody knows what they're doing

Product managers don't know what we need to make, UX don't know how our users work, engineers don't know the right way to build it. In our complex environments, we never know the total effect of what our code changes will be. There will always be errors, and there will always be changes that break the system.

We can try to slow down, and catch all the problems before we take action - this is the "waterfall" way. But agile practices recognise that this doesn't actually eliminate errors. What it usually does is pile them all up into one big batch of problems that get released all at once (discontinuous deployment) that come and ruin everyones day. And then this then becomes harder to fix quickly as you've optimised your development stream for slowness and checking.

What agile proposes is two fold. First up - test the hell out of everything with TDD. This acts as a good first pass at preventing regressions. Then we propose the following: if errors are a constant, then you break the exposure of the system to the errors down into small pieces - continuous delivery. This means that, if something goes wrong, it won't be a collection of bugs leading to a massive outage - it should be a smaller problem. And with your superb continuous delivery pipeline in place, when the problem does happen, you can fix it.

Great, so why is Tim telling me that no submissions have been added since 4 o'clock yesterday?

## Three Data Points

If I ever bothered to read the Google SRE book (it's long, forgive me) I'd probably see that there are (at least) three important times to note for an incident. An "incident" here is just a euphamism for a time that something went (or almost went) horribly wrong. There is never a good "incident", is there?

We care about:

1. When did it go wrong
2. When did you notice it went wrong
3. When did it get fixed

And we have fancy names. Time To Detection, and Time To Recovery.

So if Tim is telling me there have been no submissions since 4 o'clock, well, that's my Time To Detection. And 4 o'clock is when it all went wrong. And if _Tim_ is telling me this before I know about it, that tells me that I need to do some work on how I'm detecting stuff, because I _know_ I _should_ be able to know this stuff before Tim. Ideally I should know a few minutes after deployment.

## Observability

Which my app does have - metrics coming out of the wazoo in fact. But if those metrics aren't producing alerts, then they're reliant onn someone staring at them all day to detect that things have gone wrong. What you need is some alert that tells you that no submission has been sent in the last five minutes. This is easy to do in Grafana or whatever.

But sometimes I've got the alert but I didn't do anything about it. Why? Well, because my Teams/Slack/Whatever has an Alerts channel for my team and _it's always full of alerts_. Seriously, if the fire alarm is always going off, you're just going to stop paying it any attention. So a quick tip there - clean up your alerts channel so that if something goes in there then it's a genuine, exceptional event that needs action so that it becomes harder to ignore.

> KEY TAKEAWAY: ALERTS ARE IMPORTANT AND SHOULD NOT BE IGNORABLE

So let's pretend this is now happening. How do I know it's getting better? Do we just use vibes? Do I just count the times this month that Tim has told me about thing being horribly borked? Well, maybe that's a start. But what you really want is a way to say that this is how quickly we can detect an outage on average _this month_ and look, it got better (or worse) than _that month_. Or week or year or whatever.

You need to know your Mean Time To Detection (MTTD), and your Mean Time To Recovery (MTTR).

For years I just did not care about these numbers, and if they ever came up in conversation I would shrug internally and say to myself "well, sure, interesting, but how do you get that information". I'm not sure why I remained this dumb for so long. But maybe it was the fifteenth time that Tim told me that my whole system was broken that tipped me over the edge. Humiliation has its own motivating impulse.

All we need to do is just note the three key times - start, detection, recovery - in each post mortem in a nice structured way - slam some YAML frontmatter in yer markdown. And then you just aggregate to work out your MTTR and MTTD. Simple

## Two refinements

First up, it's probably worth splitting it all down by the severity of the incident. Under the P1-4 model which is also probably sitting in that damn SRE book uread, you break up incidents into severities based on how many people were affected, and how bad the badness was.

This is important because if it affects everyone and it's really bad, then I expect detection time to be low. But if it just affects Gary in Indiana and it means he's forced to use dark mode all day, then I really don't think I need to know about it immediately.

So we want to stick something into the incident report to say exactly how bad it was. Happily we already have this in place - the incident matrix thing. I can't remember if it has a proper name. But it's the one that says if it affects everyone and it's really bad, then it's called a P1, and if it affects one person and it doesn't stop them doing what they need to do, then it's a P4.

Of course, the tricky bit here is all the ones inbetween. What if it's for _some_ of the people and it stops them from doing what they need to do? What if it's for everyone, but it's just some colours looking bad?

## So what do I think?

I think if we start capturing these four metrics in our incident reports / post-mortems (along with tagging by which team was responsible) then we'll start having some numbers we can attack. 

I'll start to know _concretely_ exactly how long it takes me to notice a P1. And a P4, if we're consistent about writing incident reports for that level of outage.

To make the data easy to aggregate, I'm suggesting that the incident reports in CA (all markdown files) start with a bit of YAML frontmatter with those five fields in. And to help enforce that we'll have a small cli tool to help write the post-mortems, and possibly some linting to enforce that it's there.
